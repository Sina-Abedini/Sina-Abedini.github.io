Project Information

  • Title: Structure of the Bacterial Magnesium Transporter CorB by Cryo-EM Single-Particle Analysis
  • Supervisor: Dr. Kalle Gehring
  • Project Date: Sep 2023 - Present

Project Summary

Magnesium (Mg2+) is the most abundant divalent cation in cells and is involved in a myriad of cellular processes, ranging from enzymatic activity to cell membrane stabilization to neural transmission. Despite its importance, the mechanisms of Mg2+ transport and homeostasis are not fully understood.

CNNM (Cystathionine-β-synthase (CBS)-pair domain divalent cation transport mediators) proteins are an evolutionarily conserved family of cation transport proteins that play key roles in Mg2+ homeostasis across all domains of life. Human CNNMs (hCNNM) include CNNM1-4 and are expressed throughout the body. Mutations in CNNM proteins disrupt magnesium homeostasis, leading to various diseases, such as cancer and hypomagnesemia. All CNNM proteins consist of three conserved structural domains: a transmembrane domain (TMD) for ion transport, an acidic helical bundle (AHB) that likely regulates transport through Mg2+ interactions, and a CBS-pair domain that binds Mg2+-ATP. Beyond this conserved core, eukaryotic and prokaryotic CNNMs differ. In eukaryotes, CNNMs feature an N-terminal extracellular domain critical for membrane localization and transport activity, as well as a cytosolic C-terminal cyclic-nucleotide binding homology (CNBH) domain. In prokaryotes, the smaller C-terminal domain is referred to as CorC and lacks sequence similarity to CNBH domains. It has been established that CNNMs mediate ion translocation, but little is known of the mechanism and its regulation. At present, the structure of the TMD is available in only the inward-facing conformation, providing a single snapshot of the transport cycle. To fully understand the transport mechanism, assuming CNNMs operate via a rocker-switch mechanism, structural data from multiple conformations will be required. We hypothesize that an outward-facing conformation exists and aim to determine the structure using cryo-Electron Microscopy (EM).

My research focuses on using cryo-EM to capture the outward-facing conformation of the CNNM protein using its prokaryotic ortholog from the thermophilic bacterium Tepidiphilus thermophilus (TtCorB), which shares 25% sequence identity with hCNNM2. The main problem with this technique is that high-resolution structure determination of small (<100 kDa) proteins, such as CorB, remains difficult due to the challenges of particle alignment caused by flexible cytosolic domains and the absence of distinct structural features. One way to circumvent this problem is to employ protein engineering techniques to add mass and stability to the target protein. For my MSc project, I will use a synthetic antibody (Fab) in complex with a nanobody (NB; the VHH region of Fabs) as fiducial marks that add mass to the target protein, stabilize it, and assist in its orientation determination. This can be achieved by the fusion of an engineered thermostable variant of apocytochrome b562 named BRIL into the TtCorB protein. This BRIL insertion serves as a binding site for a NB-stabilized, anti-BRIL Fab. The 12kDa BRIL bound to the 50kDa Fab and the additional 16kDa added by the NB will add sufficient mass and asymmetry to the target protein to facilitate a high-resolution structure determination.

I have successfully purified the NB and the first CorB-BRIL construct, CorB-171-BRIL, which binds the purified anti-BRIL Fab. Next, I incubated the NB and the CorB-BRIL:Fab to obtain the final complex. I have also identified additional sites in the CorB structure for BRIL insertions and plan to screen for the best expressing and most stable constructs to optimize the sample for cryo-EM analysis. Once the structure and the transport mechanism of TtCorB are elucidated, they can serve as a basis for fully understanding the structure and function of human CNNMs, paving the way for designing small-molecule drugs with potential applications in cancer treatment.
